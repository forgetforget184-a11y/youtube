# The Billionaires Who Cornered the Silver Market

In nineteen seventy-nine, two brothers from Texas walked into Wall Street with an idea so crazy that nobody believed they were serious. They decided that paper money was completely worthless, that the government was heading for bankruptcy, and that the only safe place to put their wealth was inside cold, hard metal. But they did not just buy a little bit. Over twelve months, these two brothers secretly bought one-third of all the privately held silver on planet Earth. They pushed the price of silver up by more than seven hundred percent. They caused people to tear down antique candelabras and melt family heirlooms on street corners. And then, just when they thought they owned the world, the men who ran the financial system did something the brothers never saw coming. They simply changed the rules.

## The Gambler's Sons
To understand how anyone could attempt something this absurd, you first have to meet their father. His name was Haroldson Lafayette Hunt, better known across Texas simply as H. L. Hunt. In the nineteen thirties, H. L. was quite literally the richest private citizen in the world. He started out as a professional poker player wandering through dusty gambling dens in Arkansas and Louisiana. One afternoon, he won a tiny slice of an oil lease in a card game. He took that lease, traded it for another, and eventually bought the legendary East Texas Oil Field from a broke wildcatter for pennies on the dollar. 

H. L. Hunt was brilliant, completely eccentric, and deeply paranoid. He carried his lunch in a brown paper bag, drove an old beat-up car, and firmly believed that paper money was a government scam designed to steal wealth from hardworking families. When he died in nineteen seventy-four, he left behind billions of dollars and a large family. Standing right at the head of the table was his eldest son, Nelson Bunker Hunt, alongside his younger brother, William Herbert Hunt. 

Bunker Hunt was a massive, quiet man who wore rumpled off-the-rack suits and looked more like a sleepy cattle rancher than a financial titan. But underneath that calm Texas drawl was his father's absolute obsession with hard assets. Bunker inherited a personal fortune in oil fields, massive cattle ranches, and prize-winning racehorses. But in nineteen seventy-three, disaster struck. Bunker had invested heavily in a massive oil field in Libya. Out of nowhere, Colonel Muammar Gaddafi led a military coup, seized power, and nationalized Bunker's entire oil operation without paying him a single dime. 

The Libyan seizure shook Bunker Hunt to his core. He realized that a government could wipe out decades of work with the stroke of a pen. At the exact same time, back home in America, inflation was spiraling out of control. President Richard Nixon had just severed the final link between the dollar and gold. Prices for bread, milk, and gasoline were soaring, and paper money seemed to be melting away like ice under the Texas sun. Bunker turned to his brother Herbert and told him that if they kept their wealth in American dollars, they were going to end up broke. They needed something real.

## The Shiny Metal Loophole
Naturally, the first thing the Hunt brothers wanted to buy was gold. Gold had been the ultimate store of value for thousands of years. But there was a gigantic legal roadblock. Ever since President Franklin Roosevelt signed an executive order during the Great Depression, it was strictly illegal for private American citizens to own gold bullion. The government would throw you in federal prison if you hoarded gold bars in your basement. 

So Bunker and Herbert began looking for an alternative. And sitting right in plain sight was gold's forgotten little brother: silver. 

Silver was completely legal for any American citizen to buy and hold. Even better, it was shockingly cheap. Throughout the early nineteen seventies, silver traded for around one dollar and fifty cents an ounce. But Bunker Hunt was not just looking at the price tag. He started studying the global supply chain, and what he discovered blew his mind. 

Back then, the world was consuming far more silver every single year than all the silver mines on Earth were digging up. The photographic giant Eastman Kodak was using millions of ounces of silver every year to manufacture camera film. Electronics manufacturers needed silver for electrical switches. Medical companies needed silver for X-ray sheets. The gap between supply and demand was being filled by governments dumping their old silver coins and stockpiles into the market. But those government reserves were running dry. 

Bunker Hunt ran the numbers and came to an astonishing conclusion. If someone with enough cash stepped into the market and bought up the remaining supply, the entire global industrial machine would have to come begging to them for metal. 

In nineteen seventy-three, the Hunt brothers made their first move. They quietly purchased twenty million ounces of physical silver at roughly three dollars an ounce. But the brothers knew their own fortune, as vast as it was, could only push the market so far. They needed allies with deep pockets and the exact same hatred for paper currency. 

So Bunker Hunt boarded an airplane and flew straight to the Middle East. He met with members of the Saudi royal family, including Prince Mohammed bin Fahd. Bunker laid out his theory. He told the Saudis that Western paper currencies were doomed to collapse from runaway inflation, and that together, they could pool their petrodollars and build an unbeatable position in real silver. The Saudis loved the idea. Together, they formed a secret offshore partnership called the International Metals Investment Company. The trap was set, and the squeeze was about to begin.

## The Midnight Airlift
To understand how the Hunt brothers pulled off their scheme, you have to understand how commodity futures exchanges actually work. Normally, when big hedge funds or corporations trade silver on exchanges like the Chicago Board of Trade or the New York Commodity Exchange, nobody actually expects to see a single ounce of real metal. 

Instead, traders buy futures contracts. A futures contract is simply a legal agreement to buy a certain amount of silver at a specific price on a future date. But when that delivery date arrives, ninety-nine percent of traders do not want heavy silver bars dumped on their front lawn. They simply settle the contract in cash, take their paper profit or loss, and roll over into the next month. 

The Hunt brothers, however, had zero interest in paper profits. They did not want paper cash. They wanted the metal. 

When their contracts expired, the Hunts did something that sent shockwaves through the trading pits. They demanded physical delivery. They told the exchange: do not wire us dollars, bring us our silver. 

Suddenly, armored trucks began backing up to exchange warehouses across New York and Chicago, loading up thousands of heavy thirty-two-pound bars of solid silver. But Bunker Hunt was terrified the United States government might try to seize his metal, just like Roosevelt had seized gold forty years earlier. So he decided to take the silver completely out of the country. 

In the dead of winter, under the cover of darkness, Bunker Hunt chartered three Boeing seven hundred and seven cargo jets. He hired a squad of armed Texas ranch hands, dressed in cowboy boots and heavy jackets, to guard the loading docks. Pallet after pallet of solid silver bullion was loaded onto the planes. The jets took off into the night and flew straight across the Atlantic Ocean, touching down in Zurich and London. The silver was quietly locked away inside deep Swiss bank vaults. 

By nineteen seventy-nine, the Hunts and their Saudi partners were not just buying silver with cash. They were using leverage. They would take their physical silver bars, pledge them as collateral to borrow millions of dollars from Wall Street banks, and then use that borrowed cash to buy even more futures contracts. It was an unstoppable compounding loop. By late nineteen seventy-nine, the Hunt brothers controlled an estimated two hundred million ounces of silver. That was roughly half of all the deliverable silver in the entire Western world.

## The Silver Fever of Nineteen Eighty
With hundreds of millions of ounces locked away in private vaults, the open market suddenly experienced an extreme shortage. Industrial companies that desperately needed silver for film, electronics, and medicine could not find enough bars to buy. The price of silver went completely berserk. 

In January of nineteen seventy-nine, silver was trading around six dollars an ounce. By September, it hit eleven dollars. By December, it blew past twenty-five dollars. And then, in the first two weeks of January nineteen eighty, the market entered full-blown speculative hysteria. On January eighteenth, nineteen eighty, the price of silver touched an all-time record high of fifty dollars and thirty-five cents an ounce. 

In just one year, the price had skyrocketed by more than seven hundred percent. On paper, Bunker and Herbert Hunt were now sitting on an unrealized profit of several billion dollars. They were, for a few brief weeks, arguably the wealthiest private individuals on Earth. 

Across the United States, regular citizens went into a state of total mania. People suddenly realized that the dusty antique tea sets sitting in their dining rooms and the silver spoons inherited from their grandparents were worth thousands of dollars. Lines stretched around the block outside jewelry shops, pawn shops, and coin dealers in every major American city. People stood in the freezing winter cold for hours, carrying pillowcases stuffed with silverware, communion plates, and even silver dental fillings, waiting to have them weighed and paid out in cash. 

Refineries worked around the clock, melting down irreplaceable nineteenth-century heirlooms into crude silver bricks. Burglars began breaking into suburban homes, ignoring televisions and stereo equipment, and heading straight for the dining room sideboard to steal the silverware. 

The iconic luxury jeweler Tiffany and Company was so outraged by the skyrocketing price that their chairman took out a full-page advertisement in The New York Times. The ad publicly blasted the Hunt brothers, calling their actions unconscionable and accusing them of hoarding a vital public resource for private greed. But Bunker Hunt simply laughed off the criticism. When a reporter asked him how it felt to risk his fortune on silver, Bunker calmly replied that a billion dollars just was not what it used to be.

## Changing the Rules
While the Hunt brothers were celebrating their paper billions, panic was sweeping through the executive suites of Wall Street and the commodity exchanges. 

The leaders of the Chicago Board of Trade and the Commodity Exchange in New York were staring into a financial abyss. Many of the exchange board members and major trading firms were on the short side of the trade, meaning they had bet that the price of silver would go down. If the Hunts continued demanding physical delivery, there literally was not enough silver on planet Earth to satisfy the contracts. The short sellers would go bankrupt, clearinghouses would fail, and the entire commodities trading system could collapse. 

So the men in charge decided to do the only thing that could save them. They changed the rules of the game in the middle of the match. 

First, the exchanges dramatically increased the margin requirements. Previously, an investor only needed to put down a few thousand dollars in cash to control a hundred-thousand-dollar contract of silver. The exchanges suddenly demanded that traders put up tens of thousands of dollars in cash for every single contract. 

Then came the kill shot. The exchanges enacted a brand-new emergency regulation known as liquidation only. Under this new rule, nobody in the world was allowed to buy new silver futures contracts. You were only allowed to sell existing contracts to close them out. 

Think about that for a second. Imagine an auction house where buyers are banned from making any bids, and sellers are only allowed to sell. With buyers legally kicked out of the room, there was only one direction the price could possibly go. 

To make matters worse, the Federal Reserve stepped in. Chairman Paul Volcker, who was aggressively battling national inflation, issued a direct warning to commercial banks across the country. He told them to immediately stop extending credit and loans for speculative commodity hoarding. Overnight, the Hunt brothers' financial pipeline was completely shut off. They could no longer borrow fresh cash against their silver, and the market they thought they controlled was suddenly rigged against them.

## Silver Thursday and the Fall
As soon as the new rules took effect, the price of silver began to slide backward. From fifty dollars, it dropped to forty. Then thirty. Then twenty-five. 

Because the Hunt brothers had built their empire on borrowed money, every dollar drop in the price of silver triggered an avalanche of margin calls. A margin call is when a broker calls you on the telephone and demands that you deposit millions of dollars in fresh cash immediately to cover your losses. If you cannot produce the cash, the broker has the legal right to sell your assets at any price they can get. 

For several weeks, the Hunts scrambled. They poured in millions of dollars of their own cash, sold off family assets, and tried to negotiate new loans. But the decline was relentless. 

Everything came to a catastrophic head on the morning of March twenty-seventh, nineteen eighty, a day that financial history will forever remember as Silver Thursday. 

That morning, the major brokerage firm Bache Halsey Stuart Shields picked up the telephone and issued a massive margin call to the Hunt brothers for one hundred million dollars. The Hunts simply did not have the cash. Bunker Hunt was overseas in Europe trying to arrange emergency financing, but the clock ran out. Bache immediately began liquidating the Hunts' silver positions on the open floor, dumping millions of ounces onto a market that had zero buyers. 

The trading pits erupted in absolute pandemonium. The price of silver went into freefall, plunging from twenty-one dollars down to ten dollars and eighty cents in a matter of hours. Rumors spread like wildfire across Wall Street that major brokerage firms were failing and that the Hunt brothers were insolvent. The Dow Jones Industrial Average plunged, and trading in several banking stocks was temporarily halted. 

Terrified of a systemic banking collapse, Federal Reserve officials and a syndicate of thirteen major commercial banks rushed into emergency meetings. Over the coming weeks, they put together a one-point-one-billion-dollar emergency credit package to bail out the brokerage firms and prevent a domino collapse of the financial system. But in exchange, the banks took total control of the Hunts. The brothers were forced to pledge their vast oil properties, real estate, ranches, racehorses, and family holdings as collateral. The silver empire had turned into a financial prison.

## The Billion-Dollar Lesson
The fallout from the silver squeeze dragged on for nearly a decade. In nineteen eighty-eight, a federal jury found Nelson Bunker Hunt and William Herbert Hunt guilty of civil conspiracy to corner the silver market. The jury ordered the brothers to pay over one hundred and thirty million dollars in damages to a Peruvian state-owned mineral company that had been caught in the crossfire. 

Faced with astronomical legal bills, bank debts, and penalties from the Internal Revenue Service, both Bunker and Herbert Hunt were forced to file for Chapter eleven personal bankruptcy. At their bankruptcy hearings, Bunker Hunt sat quietly in a crowded courtroom while auctioneers sold off his prize racehorses, his father's oil leases, and even his personal coin collections to satisfy creditors. His personal fortune, once estimated to be well over five billion dollars, had almost completely vanished. 

Decades later, the Hunt brothers' silver gamble stands as one of the most audacious attempts in history to corner a global commodity. They were right about inflation. They were right that industrial demand was outstripping mine supply. And they were right that paper currency loses purchasing power over time. 

But they forgot the most important rule of high-stakes finance: when the little guy breaks the rules, he goes to jail. But when the big guy breaks the house, the house simply rewrites the rules.
